Sex toy wholesale: a complete guide for new distributors

Wholesale in this category is not complicated because the products are unusual. It is complicated because the supply chain is opaque, the paperwork is inconsistent, and almost every supplier will quote you a number that hides the cost of getting goods to your shelf. This guide fixes the order of operations.

On this page 1. How the supply chain actually works 2. The five numbers to calculate first 3. What a first order should look like 4. Factory, trading company or consolidator 5. The contract terms that decide your margin 6. Red flags that cost real money

1. How the supply chain actually works

There are four layers, and most buyers only see two of them.

  • Component makers — silicone moulding, motors, PCBs, batteries. They sell to product makers, rarely to brands.
  • Product makers — assemble and test the finished item. They own the mould and set the real MOQ.
  • Trading companies and consolidators — buy from several product makers, hold stock, handle export documents and mixed cartons. This is the layer most overseas buyers actually deal with, including us.
  • Brands and distributors — that is you, adding positioning, packaging, compliance responsibility and the customer relationship.

What matters commercially: the further left you buy, the lower the price and the higher the MOQ. The further right, the more you pay per unit but the less cash and risk you carry. A new distributor buying 200 pieces should not be talking to a mould owner who needs 3,000.

2. The five numbers to calculate first

Before you email anyone, build these five numbers. They decide whether a quote is good or merely cheap.

  1. Realistic retail price in your market, after tax and platform fees — not the price you hope to charge.
  2. Landed cost per unit = ex-works price + packing + freight + duty + payment fees + your defect allowance. The landed cost guide walks through a full example.
  3. Expected sell-through per month per SKU. If you cannot name a number, buy fewer SKUs, not more stock.
  4. Cash cycle — days from payment to your first sale, and days until the stock is sold. Wholesale is a cash-flow business before it is a margin business.
  5. Defect allowance — 1–3% of units is normal in this category; plan for replacement cost, not for an argument.
A 45% gross margin on paper can turn into 20% real margin once freight, duty and two replaced units are in the calculation. Build the model before you negotiate the price.

3. What a first order should look like

The right first order is small enough that a mistake is survivable, and large enough that a supplier treats you as a buyer rather than a tourist.

A practical structure for a new distributor:

  • 3–5 SKUs, not 20. Concentrate on the categories you can actually sell in your market.
  • 20–50 pieces per SKU for the two best sellers, 10–20 for the rest. This usually clears the stock-item MOQ and keeps the total under one pallet.
  • One shipment, air if the value density is high, sea only when the order exceeds roughly 1 CBM.
  • A written reorder price for the next tier — the number that matters is not the first order but the second.

Then reorder what sold. The most expensive mistake in this category is a first order that fills a warehouse with six months of unproven inventory.

4. Factory, trading company or consolidator

All three are legitimate; they fail in different ways.

ConsolidatorMixed cartons, one shipment, document handlingStock risk sits with them; less customisationMulti-SKU, multi-category orders

Ask each one the same three questions and compare answers, not prices: do you hold this stock, who owns the mould, and who issues the test report under whose name.

5. The contract terms that decide your margin

The unit price is the least important line in a wholesale agreement. These four decide whether you make money:

  • Incoterm — FOB puts freight and insurance on you; DDP puts the landed number on the supplier but hides the breakdown. Ask for the freight quote separately even when you buy DDP.
  • Tolerance — quantity and colour tolerance. A 5% short shipment at the same price is a 5% margin cut.
  • Defect replacement — replacement or credit, who pays freight, and the evidence required. Put it in writing before the deposit.
  • Price validity — 30 days is normal; 90 days with a fixed reorder tier is better.

6. Red flags that cost real money

  • No test report, only a logo on the box. A CE mark printed by the factory is artwork, not compliance. See the certification guide.
  • "Silicone" that is not silicone. TPE sold as medical silicone is the most common substitution in this category. Ask for the material report and a burn test on the sample.
  • Price that beats the market by 40%. Something is missing: motor grade, battery certification, packaging, or the QC step.
  • Payment only by personal transfer, no company invoice. You will have no recourse and no import documentation.
  • Photos of a product the supplier does not stock. Ask for a dated photo of the actual carton with your order number written on it.
If you take one thing from this page: negotiate the second order, not the first. The first order buys information; the second buys margin.
