Sourcing adult products from 1688: seven mistakes Western buyers make

1688 is a wholesale marketplace, not a factory directory. It is unbeatable for price discovery and terrible as a due-diligence tool. These are the seven mistakes we see most often from overseas buyers — including the ones that look like good deals at the quotation stage.

1. Treating the listed price as the price

Most 1688 listings show a "starting at" price for the smallest configuration: no battery, no box, one colour, sometimes a single unit. Once you specify the retail version — colour, packaging, manual, certification — the number moves 20–40%. Always quote the finished, retail-ready configuration and the carton quantity together.

2. Accepting "medical silicone" without a material report

TPE sold as silicone is the single most common substitution in this category. TPE is cheaper, softer and degrades faster; it also cannot be sold as body-safe silicone in most regulated markets without a very different claim set. Ask for the material test report and check that the model and material in the report match the product you are buying. A burn test on the sample is a useful sanity check: silicone ash is white and odourless, TPE melts and smells like plastic.

3. Missing the battery documents

Any product with a lithium battery needs a UN38.3 test summary and a battery declaration before air freight. Sellers routinely omit it because they ship by sea themselves. If you discover the gap after the goods are packed, you pay for re-testing or a slower route. Make the documents a condition of the proforma, not a request after payment.

4. Believing the sample came from the same line as the bulk

Suppliers sometimes buy a competitor's product as the "sample" and then manufacture their own version for the bulk order. The differences show up in motor noise, button feel, seam quality and packaging weight. Defend yourself with a golden sample: keep the approved sample, photograph it with a dated label, and write into the PO that bulk must match it, with AQL inspection against that sample.

5. Playing the MOQ game

Listing MOQ is often 1 piece because the seller is a distributor, not a maker. That is fine for samples and terrible for consistency: the second order may come from a different factory with a different motor. Ask a direct question: do you manufacture this, or do you buy it? If they buy it, ask who makes it and whether they can hold stock for a reorder.

6. Skipping the QC step

At 200 pieces, a 3% defect rate is six units — acceptable. At 2,000 pieces, the same rate is 60 units, and if they arrive dead the argument costs more than the inspection. Book a third-party AQL inspection or require dated QC photos and a function-test video of the packed cartons before the balance payment. The cost is small relative to the risk.

7. Ignoring export packing

Domestic cartons are designed for pallets in a Chinese warehouse, not for air freight and last-mile handling in your market. Expect crushed corners, unmarked cartons and missing carton labels. Specify export-grade cartons, carton marks, and a packing list that matches the physical cartons. If the supplier cannot do it, a consolidator can — which is exactly the service layer we provide.

A useful rule: every claim on 1688 is a hypothesis until it appears in a document, a dated photo, or a sample you physically hold. Budget one sample cycle specifically to test the supplier, not the product.

What good sourcing looks like

  • one written spec sheet per SKU, including material, motor, battery and packaging;
  • a document pack per model, not per supplier;
  • a golden sample kept on both sides;
  • a QC step before balance payment;
  • a reorder price agreed before the first order ships.
