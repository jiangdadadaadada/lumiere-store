Discreet packaging and customs: shipping adult goods legally

"Discreet" sells products, and it is also the area where sellers get into trouble fastest, because the line between discreet and misdeclared is thin. This is where the line actually sits.

Discreet is a packaging decision, not a customs decision

What is normally acceptable, and what buyers expect:

  • a plain, opaque outer carton with no brand name and no product words;
  • a neutral shipper name and address on the label;
  • no product photography or category wording on the outside;
  • a commercial invoice inside or attached in a document pouch, not printed on the box.

What is not acceptable — and increasingly detected by carrier and customs data matching:

  • declaring "plastic parts" or "gift" for a shipment of finished electronic products;
  • under-invoicing the value to reduce duty;
  • omitting a lithium battery declaration because the words on the invoice sound harmless.
Discretion protects the recipient's privacy. It does not reduce the legal description of the goods. A neutral carton with an accurate invoice is discreet and legal; a vague invoice is neither, once customs opens it.

The documents that keep a shipment moving

  1. Commercial invoice — accurate description, HS code, unit value, country of origin, terms of sale.
  2. Packing list — cartons, quantities, gross and net weight, dimensions, matching the physical labels.
  3. HS classification — confirmed with your broker for the destination. Adult products do not have one universal code; classification depends on function, material and whether electronics are present.
  4. Battery documents — UN38.3 test summary and a battery declaration for air shipments.
  5. Test reports / declarations — where the destination authority or marketplace asks for them.
  6. Certificate of origin — when a preferential tariff applies.

Some markets restrict the goods themselves

Before quoting a destination, check whether adult products are restricted, require an import licence, or are prohibited outright. A handful of markets treat them as restricted or banned goods, and some carriers refuse them regardless of legality. The consequence of getting this wrong is not a delay — it is seizure and a destroyed shipment, with no insurance payout if the goods were misdeclared.

Practical rule: confirm three things per destination before booking freight — import permission, carrier acceptance, and the HS code your broker will use.

Who clears customs, and who carries the risk

TermYou arrangeSupplier arrangesBest for
FOBFreight, insurance, clearance, dutyExport clearance, loadingBuyers with their own forwarder
CIFClearance, duty, last mileFreight and insurance to portSea freight, experienced importers
DDPNothing — a landed priceEverything, including dutySmall orders, new importers, marketplaces
DDUDuty and clearance on arrivalDelivery to your doorBuyers who want duty visibility

DDP is convenient and hides the cost breakdown; ask for the duty line anyway. If a DDP quote is materially below the duty you calculated yourself, the declaration is being shaped to fit the price.

Last mile and age rules

  • Some carriers offer pickup-point delivery that avoids a doorstep conversation — useful for consumer orders, irrelevant for pallets.
  • Age-restricted delivery rules differ by market; marketplaces often impose stricter checks than the law.
  • Your invoice and labelling must identify the importer of record — in the EU, that is also the responsible economic operator for compliance.
Insurance is only valid when the declared goods match reality. A neutral carton is covered; a disguised invoice is not.
