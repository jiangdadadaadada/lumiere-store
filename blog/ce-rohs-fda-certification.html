CE, RoHS, FDA, REACH: which certificates a buyer really needs

In this category, "certified" is used to mean four different things: a mark printed on a box, a test report for a material, a declaration signed by someone, and a registration filed with a regulator. They are not interchangeable, and the obligation usually sits with the importer — you.

What each one actually covers

There is no dedicated EU directive for "sex toys". CE for a vibrator is usually assembled from general product safety rules plus EMC, RoHS and battery rules where the product has electronics. That is why a CE certificate PDF with no directive references is usually worthless.

How CE really works

CE is a self-declaration system for most products in this category. The manufacturer writes a declaration of conformity listing the directives and standards applied, keeps a technical file, and puts the mark on the product. A lab test supports the file; it does not replace it.

Three things an importer should hold:

  • the declaration of conformity naming the model, the directives and the standards;
  • the test report, with the sample clearly identified as the same model and material;
  • the technical file — drawings, bill of materials, instructions, and the risk assessment.

If the supplier can only produce a certificate with a logo and no standards, ask for the declaration instead. If they cannot produce that either, the product is not compliant, regardless of what is printed on the box.

The US is different

Adult toys are generally not regulated as FDA medical devices, and there is no 510(k) pathway for a vibrator. FDA requirements become relevant when:

  • the product is a personal lubricant — classification depends on claims (cosmetic versus drug);
  • the marketing makes medical claims — therapeutic, curative or diagnostic language can push a product into device territory;
  • the product is marketed for children — a different and much stricter regime applies.

Separately, California Proposition 65 warnings and state-level chemical rules can apply to materials, and marketplaces run their own document checks that are stricter than the law.

Three questions that expose a fake report

  1. Which standard, exactly? A real report cites standard numbers and editions — for example the EMC and safety standards applied, or the specific phthalate list tested under REACH.
  2. Which sample? The report must identify the model and material tested. A report for "silicone sheet, generic" does not cover your finished product.
  3. Which lab, and is it accredited? Look for an accreditation reference (CNAS, CMA, ILAC-MRA) and a report number you can verify with the lab, not just with the supplier.

Who carries the obligation after the shipment arrives

Under EU rules the importer is responsible for the product being compliant, even when the factory produced the report. Practical consequences for wholesale buyers:

  • your company name and address must appear on the product or packaging as the EU responsible person;
  • you need the technical file, not a link to one;
  • if the factory changes material or motor without telling you, the documents no longer match the goods in your warehouse.
Put document control in the purchase order: model, material, motor and battery part numbers, and a clause that any change requires a new test report before shipment. That single clause prevents most compliance surprises.
