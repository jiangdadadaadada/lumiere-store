MOQ and tiered pricing: how to calculate your landed cost

Wholesale quotes are usually quoted ex-works or FOB, and buyers compare those numbers as if they were final. They are not. Here is the full build on a realistic 200-piece order, with every line that belongs in it.

Goods were 64% of the landed cost. Unit landed cost is USD 13.29 average, but it is not uniform: the mid-size vibrator carries roughly USD 19.50 while the lubricant lands at about USD 4.10. If you price every SKU with the average markup, the heavy items lose money and the light ones look artificially profitable.

Why MOQ tiers exist

Tiered pricing is not a negotiation trick. It reflects real cost behaviour:

  • Setup — colour change, printing plates, packing configuration. Fixed per run.
  • Freight step — below about 100 kg, air is usually the only sensible option; above roughly 1 CBM, sea freight cuts cost per unit sharply but adds 25–40 days.
  • Factory slot — at 200+ pieces a supplier can plan production; at 10 pieces it ships from stock, which costs more per unit but requires no commitment.

That is why a quote should always show the next tier: the question is not "what is the price at 50 pieces", it is "what happens to the price at 200".

The number to plan around

Three ratios, calculated from the landed cost, keep a wholesale plan honest:

  1. Landed-to-retail — landed cost should sit at 30–45% of your realistic retail price, after platform fees and tax.
  2. Cash coverage — landed total ÷ expected monthly sell-through value. Under 2 months, you are safe; over 4, you are financing inventory.
  3. Reorder trigger — reorder when stock covers 6 weeks, not when it hits zero, because restock is 3–7 days by air and 30–45 by sea.
A supplier who will not break the price down into goods, freight and packing is not hiding the unit price — they are hiding the freight. Ask for the split.
